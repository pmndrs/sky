title: Night sky
description: Pixel-sharp stars and a Milky Way that rise with the real sky, fade in through twilight, and cost almost nothing per frame.
---

```js
import { Sky } from '@pmndrs/sky'

const sky = new Sky(renderer, { timeOfDay: 21.25, dayOfYear: 196, latitude: 38 })
sky.attach(scene)
const night = await sky.enableStars()

renderer.setAnimationLoop(() => {
  sky.update(camera) // bakes when dirty, and moves the star sprites with the camera
  renderer.render(scene, camera)
})
```

That is the whole setup. The stars and the Milky Way appear on their own as
the sun goes down and leave again at dawn — there is no day/night switch to
manage.

## What you get

The night sky is split by frequency, because a point of light and a diffuse
glow need opposite treatment:

- **Stars** are drawn as sprites in _your_ scene (`SkyStars`, added by
  `attach()`, also when it claims only the background or only the
  environment). Each is a small energy-normalised Gaussian, so it stays sharp
  at any resolution, doesn't shimmer as the camera turns, and faint stars are
  faint rather than tiny. About 9,000 stars — roughly what the eye sees on a
  dark night — with the brightest ones at their real positions.
- **The Milky Way** is a low-resolution glow map sampled by the sky shader and
  baked into the sky cube with everything else. It shows up in
  `scene.background`, in reflections, and (faintly) in the environment
  lighting.

Stars are **not** in the cube: at the cube's resolution a star smears into a
blurry blob several pixels wide.

## Lighting the night

For a coordinated game-style night, use the opt-in moonlighting helper:

```js
const moonlight = sky.createMoonlight({ phase: 0.5 }).attach(scene)
moonlight.fitShadowToBox(landscapeBounds) // Box3 around the area that needs shadows
renderer.shadowMap.enabled = true

moonlight.setPhase(0.25) // quarter: a half-disc, with weaker lighting
moonlight.setIntensity(1.5) // overall light and fill strength
```

It owns a shadow-casting moon directional light and its visible disc, a gentle
hemisphere fill, and a temporary blue night sky grade. They fade through dawn
and follow moon phase and visibility. The night grade also reaches reflections
and environment lighting through the existing cube bake; no second atmospheric
LUT is needed. Star visibility follows the graded sky, so faint stars emerge
as the moonlight fades.

`moonlight.moon` exposes the underlying `SkyMoon`, including disc color, size,
and brightness. Its phase mask shows waxing and waning crescents, full moon,
and a dark new moon. Light strength uses the **square of the illuminated
fraction**, an artistic curve: full moon = 1, quarter = 0.25, new = 0.
Moonrise and moonset use the visible disc and the camera's local planet horizon.

The default moonless floor retains 15% of the fill so shadows remain readable.
It is an artistic choice, adjustable with `setMoonlessFloor(0..1)`. Direct
moonlight turns off when the moon sets; the floor remains. `setMoonIntensity`,
`setAmbientIntensity`, and `setSkyBrightness` adjust the three layers separately.
Disc radiance remains independently adjustable with `moonlight.moon.setDiscIntensity`.

The helper temporarily replaces the assigned grade and restores it on
`detach()` or `dispose()`, provided it still owns that assignment. Pass
`skyFill: false` to keep a custom sky grade. It does not change the renderer's
exposure; use `toneMappingExposure` to tune the scene. The helper is caller-owned:
dispose it before disposing the sky. Fit shadow bounds around the foreground
area, rather than including a giant ground or sky mesh; the bounds automatically
follow moon direction changes.

`examples/vanilla/14-night-sky.html` is the moonlit landscape demo. Run
`pnpm example:vanilla` to compare phases, moonset, and the original dark sky.
The demo starts at midnight with automatic moon movement, like the API.
Editing moon elevation or azimuth switches to manual placement; `followSun`
restores the automatic orbit, and `lookAtMoon` points the camera at it.
The demo uses a larger 1.2° disc for visibility; the API defaults to 0.535°.

### Moon visibility in daylight

The moon can be visible during the day, especially near quarter phase;
its visibility depends on phase, time of year and location
([NASA's moon phase guide](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/)).
Automatic placement keeps the full moon opposite the sun and places quarters
around 90° away. The current orbit is a phase-based approximation, not a lunar
ephemeris; date and latitude follow the solar model, while you author phase.

`daylightFade` is enabled by default. The disc retains the baked sky color
in front of its surface and reduces its cinematic brightness boost in daylight.
Its opacity follows contrast with the local sky, so it remains pale and visible
in clear conditions and washes out in bright haze. Transmittance and the planet
mask still handle reddening, dimming and moonset. There is no clock-hour cutoff.
This is artistic contrast adaptation, not a physical eye or lunar-reflectance model.

For a deliberately glowing moon, use `moon.setDaylightFade(false)` or the
`daylightFade` demo toggle. Disc intensity and color remain independently tunable.

For independent artistic controls, the existing ambient and grade helpers
remain available:

```js
sky.createAmbient().attach(scene) // hemisphere fill light, fades in after sunset
sky.setGrade('blue-night') // a deep-blue sky fill, keyed on the sun, in the cube and IBL
```

Both are covered in [Sky grades](./sky-grades#ambient-light). Stars and the
Milky Way fade against the sky behind them, so a filled sky keeps the
brightest stars and holds back the faint ones, as a real moonlit sky does.

## Visible moon

```js
const moon = sky.createMoon({ intensity: 0.15, phase: 0.5 }).attach(scene)
moon.setDiscIntensity(2)
moon.setDiscAngularDiameter(0.00935) // radians, about 0.535°
```

`moon.disc` is a mesh attached to `moon.light`. It stays sharp regardless of
sky cube resolution and follows the moon direction with no camera parallax.
Atmospheric transmittance tints and dims it at moonrise; the planet and
scene geometry occlude it. `moon.setDiscVisible(false)` hides the visual
while keeping moonlight. Disc brightness and light intensity are separate.

Standalone `createMoon` preserves its full-circle disc and constant requested
intensity by default. Pass `phaseLighting: true` (or call `setPhaseLighting(true)`)
for the same crescent mask, artistic phase brightness, and planet horizon
used by `createMoonlight`.

This setup does not make the moon illuminate the atmospheric LUTs or simulate
low-light vision in tonemapping. Those remain separate enhancements; the
current blue fill and direct lighting are intentionally authored for readability.

## Driven by the sky's time and place

Both layers are oriented from the same inputs as the sun — `timeOfDay`,
`dayOfYear`, `latitude` and `north` — using local sidereal time from the same
solar model, so the stars stay registered to the sunset (the tests hold the sun's
catalog position within 0.5° of the sky's sun):

```js
sky.setTimeOfDay(23) // the sky turns about the celestial pole
sky.setLatitude(-33.9) // southern sky
sky.setNorth('+X') // stars follow north, like the sun
sky.setNorth(25) // or any heading, clockwise from +Z seen from above
```

`setSunDirection()` moves only the sun; the stars keep following the time and
place you set.

Both layers are also dimmed and reddened toward the horizon by atmospheric
transmittance, hidden below the planet's horizon, and depth-tested against
your geometry, so they set behind terrain.

The sprites are additive and transparent, so they draw over the sky whether
it is the `scene.background` cube or a live sky mesh in the scene
(`sky.baker.createSkyMesh()`): the live mesh draws after the opaque geometry
(`SKY_RENDER_ORDER`) without writing depth, and transparent objects are a
later list still.

## Twilight

A fixed exposure has no eye adaptation, so the twilight sky renders darker
than it looks in reality and anything added on top would show far too early.
Each star, and each pixel of the Milky Way, is instead compared with the sky
directly behind it and faded in over a few stops as that sky darkens. Bright
stars appear first, the dark side of the sky fills in before the twilight
side, and the Milky Way emerges last.

`contrast` and `milkyWayContrast` move that point: higher values hold them
back until later in twilight.

## Options

`sky.enableStars(options)` (or `night.enable(options)`) updates in place;
the setters change one value without enabling a disabled night sky.

| Option              | Default | Setter                 | Effect                                                                                           |
| ------------------- | ------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `intensity`         | `1`     | `setIntensity`         | Star brightness.                                                                                 |
| `size`              | `0.7`   | `setSize`              | Star blur spot σ in CSS pixels (≈1.6 px wide at half maximum). Bright stars grow on top of this. |
| `magnitudeContrast` | `0.75`  | `setMagnitudeContrast` | `1` keeps true brightness ratios between magnitudes; lower compresses them.                      |
| `contrast`          | `40`    | `setContrast`          | Star : sky ratio at the middle of the twilight fade.                                             |
| `twinkle`           | `0.25`  | `setTwinkle`           | Scintillation depth; stronger toward the horizon.                                                |
| `count`             | `9000`  | —                      | Procedural stars (the named bright stars are extra). Rebuilds the sprites.                       |
| `seed`              | —       | —                      | Procedural field seed. Rebuilds the sprites.                                                     |
| `catalog`           | —       | —                      | Your own `StarCatalog` (see below). Rebuilds the sprites.                                        |
| `milkyWay`          | `0.25`  | `setMilkyWay`          | Milky Way intensity; `0` hides it.                                                               |
| `milkyWayContrast`  | `20`    | `setMilkyWayContrast`  | Glow : sky ratio at the middle of its twilight fade.                                             |
| `milkyWayTexture`   | —       | `setMilkyWayTexture`   | Your own map instead of the procedural one; `null` goes back.                                    |

`sky.disableStars()` hides both layers; `sky.enableStars()` brings them back.
`sky.setStarsIntensity(n)` is a shortcut for `setIntensity` and `sky.stars`
returns the `SkyNight` (or `null` before the first `enableStars`).

The options are typed as `SkyNightOptions`: `SkyStarsOptions` (the star
rows, `intensity` through `catalog`) plus the three `milkyWay*` fields.
`SkyStars` is exported on its own too —
`new SkyStars(sky.baker, options)` builds the sprite group, but then you own
its `orientation` matrix and its per-frame `update(camera, pixelRatio)`,
which `SkyNight` otherwise handles.

## Astronomy helpers

The solar model behind the sun and the star field is exported:
`solarPosition({ timeOfDay, dayOfYear, latitude })` gives the sun's
`{ elevation, azimuth }` in degrees (azimuth clockwise from north),
`solarEquatorial({ timeOfDay, dayOfYear })` its right ascension and
declination in radians, and `localSiderealTime({ timeOfDay, dayOfYear })`
the right ascension on the meridian, in radians. All take local solar hours.
`localSiderealTime` uses the same hour angle as `solarPosition`, which is
what keeps the stars registered to the sun.

## Your own Milky Way map or catalog

`milkyWayTexture` takes an equirectangular map in **galactic** coordinates:
the galactic centre at `u = 0.5` with longitude increasing with `u`, and
galactic latitude from −90° at `v = 0` to +90° at `v = 1`, in linear colour.
Most published all-sky maps (Gaia, for example) come in this layout; check
the licence before shipping one. A texture you pass in is never disposed by
the sky. `generateMilkyWayTexture({ width })` regenerates the procedural one
at another resolution.

`catalog` takes `{ directions, magnitudes, tones, count }`: unit
**equatorial** direction vectors (`x = cos δ cos α`, `y = sin δ`,
`z = cos δ sin α`), visual magnitudes, and a 0 (warm) to 1 (hot) colour per
star. `generateStarCatalog()` shows the expected shape.

## Cost

Measured on an M5 Pro, per frame, over the plain cube background:

|                              | 1920×1080 | 3840×2160    |
| ---------------------------- | --------- | ------------ |
| Stars + Milky Way (9k stars) | ~0.01 ms  | ~0.05–0.2 ms |
| 50k stars                    | ~0.05 ms  | ~0.05 ms     |
| Daytime (stars invisible)    | ~0        | ~0           |

The Milky Way lives in the cube, so it only costs anything when the cube
re-bakes. Moving the time of day re-bakes the cube anyway — that is the sun
moving — and dominates everything above: see
`examples/vanilla/16-stars-bench.html` and
`examples/vanilla/scripts/bench-stars.mjs`.

## Upgrading from 0.3

The procedural hash-grid starfield and the HDRI star map were baked into the
cube, which is what made them blurry, and are gone:

| Before                                 | Now                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `enableStars({ source: 'hdri', url })` | Load the map yourself (it must be in galactic coordinates) and pass `milkyWayTexture`. Point stars in an HDRI blur in the cube; use the sprites. |
| `density`, `setStarsDensity`           | `count`                                                                                                                                          |
| `brightness`, `setStarsBrightness`     | `intensity` / `setStarsIntensity`                                                                                                                |
| `rotation`, `setStarsRotation`         | Follows `timeOfDay`, `dayOfYear`, `latitude` and `north` automatically.                                                                          |
| `setStarsSource`                       | —                                                                                                                                                |
